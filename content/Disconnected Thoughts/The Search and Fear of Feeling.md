> [!tip] Intentionality vs Happiness and Success
> *I therefore admonish my students both in Europe and in America: 'Don't aim at success - the more you aim at it, the more you are going to miss it. For success, like happiness, cannot be pursued; it must ensue, and it only does so as the unintended side-effect of one's dedication to a cause greater than oneself or as the byproduct of one's surrender to a person other than oneself.*
>
>*Happiness must happen, and the same holds for success: you have to let it happen by not caring about it. I want you to listen to what your conscience commands you to do and go on to carry it out to the best of your knowledge. Then you will live to see that in the long run - in the long run, I say! - success will follow you precisely because you had **forgotten** to think of it*
>
>-Viktor Frankl

I've recently been reading Viktor Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning*, as I found it difficult to reconcile my decisions with the guiding principles for how I want to live my life. Nietzsche famously said that "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how", and I feel that I've spent the last few years attempting to discover that why for myself. 

Frankl posits that much of the search for meaning is itself the why people search for: the journey of finding purpose is itself the purpose of life. For the same reasons that people don't commit suicide: loved ones, jobs, dependents - are often the veins from which purpose flows. He grounds this message in his experience at Auschwitz, which highlights how even in the most unimaginable of human suffering, there are lessons to be learned of man's capacity for perseverance.

For much of my latter high school and early college life I've set forth on the path of software engineering and computer science, not because I knew that it was the right path for me, but because it was a path that I knew reasonably well how to execute. I wasn't certain that it was the path for me, but I figured that seeing this path through to 100% of my ability would reveal to either be my path, or quickly reveal that it wasn't. I've always loved history and film and theatre, but I could never see my way forwards with any of them, so computer science was the safe path which I knew would be at least the 80th percentile of what I wanted to do.

I loved my passions but above all else I loved solving complex problems elegantly and intuitively, and I only saw this connection when I came to college. I'd been placed at the altar of software engineering from a young age, with *FIRST* dominating my childhood. I spent a tremendous amount of time with Python and robots, but it always felt like I was looking through a film: it wasn't chosen of my own agency, so it never truly felt mine. In college, however, I saw the tangible connection between the work other people were doing and the positive outcomes it yielded to real people. Even at a small-scale, the nonexistent barrier to entry and the speed that software solutions can work at result in one of the easiest ways to make a positive impact in people's lives. I joined my university's Formula SAE team, I threw myself into personal projects and even started tutoring the courses I'd taken starting in January 2025.

Though the disconnect between myself and the work I was doing diminished, I didn't fully understand where profession intersected between what I know about myself and what I want to do until one of my closest friends that I'd made at university said something to me.

I'd never thought about it that way, but looking back on the narrative of my life I'd always fallen madly in love with people, and more broadly speaking relationships as an abstract concept. I'd try to throw every friend I had a surprise birthday party, so that they could experience celebration on their birthday, personalized to them by the people who love them. During my time in the theatre department in high school, we had a tradition called "Feel Good Friday". Before our Friday performances, everyone would put out a sheet of paper with their name on it. Throughout the day, people would come by and write a kind note or thoughtful message on their papers. I became known for writing multiple paragraphs on people's papers, taking them all over the building to make sure people knew how I felt about them. This continued into college where I'd organize birthday parties and Secret Santa for all of my friends, going so far as to create an event I called *Formies*, where I created a Google Form of superlatives, hypothetical questions and situations about everyone in my friend group. I also made a slander video to poke fun at everyone, especially myself.
